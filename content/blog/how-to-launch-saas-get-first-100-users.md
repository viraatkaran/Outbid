Most new products do not fail because they are bad. They fail because nobody ever finds them. If you want to get your first 100 users, you do not need a large audience or a big budget. You need a clear promise, a short list of places where your customers already are, and the discipline to talk to people one at a time. This is a plan you can follow with zero followers.

## Before launch: know exactly who it is for

Write one sentence: "We help [specific person] do [specific job] without [specific pain]." If the sentence is vague, your marketing will be vague too. "We help freelance designers send invoices without chasing clients" beats "a finance tool for creatives".

Then have **ten real conversations** with people who match that sentence. Ask what they do today, what annoys them and what they have already tried. You are not selling. You are collecting the exact words your future customers use, and you will reuse those words on your website.

## Build the smallest page that can convert

You need three things on a single page:

- A headline that repeats the promise in your customers' words.
- One clear picture or short video of the product working.
- A single button, either "Start free" or "Join the waiting list".

Add a short email form. Even if you are not ready to sell, a list of interested people is your first asset, and your first 100 users will mostly come from it.

## Where your first users actually come from

Early users rarely arrive by accident. They come from places you deliberately go.

1. **Direct outreach.** Message people who fit your sentence and ask for ten minutes of feedback. Offer early access in return. Aim for twenty to thirty personal messages a week.
2. **Communities.** Find the forums, groups and channels where your customers ask for help. Be useful first: answer questions, share what you learned, and mention your product only where it genuinely fits and the rules allow it.
3. **Launch platforms and directories.** Product directories and launch sites give new products a one-off spike and a lasting link. Prepare a clear description, screenshots and a short demo.
4. **Search.** Write simple pages that answer the questions your customers type into search engines, such as comparisons and how-to guides. This is slow at first and then keeps working for you.
5. **Rankings and listings.** Paid and free listings can send a trickle of visitors who are already looking for something like you. See how businesses rank by [country and category](/countries) and decide whether a listing fits your budget.

## Launch week: a simple sequence

- **Day 1:** tell your story. What you built, why, and what is still rough. Honest posts travel further than polished ones.
- **Day 2 to 3:** email everyone who talked to you and everyone on the waiting list. Give them a reason to try it this week.
- **Day 4 to 5:** share a short demo in the communities where you are already a member.
- **Day 6 to 7:** thank people publicly, answer every message, and collect what they said.

## Make the first ten minutes count

Many people sign up and never come back. Look at your product like a stranger. How quickly can a new user reach the first useful result? Remove every step that is not needed. A short checklist or a pre-filled example often doubles the number of people who finish setup.

## Measure the few numbers that matter

Do not drown in dashboards. Track:

- Visitors to your page.
- Sign-ups, and the percentage of visitors who sign up.
- Activated users: people who reached the first useful result.
- Where each sign-up came from.

If a channel sends a lot of visitors but nobody activates, it is the wrong audience. If a channel sends few visitors but many activate, do more of it. When you pay for visibility, divide what you spent by the sign-ups it produced. Our guide to [whether paid leaderboards work](/blog/paid-leaderboards-do-they-work) shows how to do that calculation.

## Keep talking to users

After each batch of new users, message five of them and ask what almost stopped them from signing up and what they would miss if the product disappeared. Fix the top item on that list every week. Your first hundred users are the cheapest market research you will ever get.

## A realistic timeline

Expect the first ten users to take the longest, because they come from personal outreach. The next forty usually come from your launch and communities. The last fifty come from a mix of search, listings and word of mouth. If you [choose your tools carefully](/blog/best-ai-tools-for-founders-2026), you can run all of this with a very small team.
