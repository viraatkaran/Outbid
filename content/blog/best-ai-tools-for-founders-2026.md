There are thousands of AI products, and a new batch appears every week. If you are building a company, the problem is not finding the best AI tools for founders. The problem is choosing a handful that save real hours, and ignoring the rest. This guide gives you a simple method you can repeat every time a new tool lands in your feed.

## Start from the job, not the tool

Write down the three tasks that cost your team the most time each week. For most early companies the list looks something like this: answering customer questions, drafting content, researching leads, reviewing code, and reporting on numbers. Search for tools by task, not by hype.

A useful rule: if you cannot describe the job in one sentence, you are not ready to buy a tool for it. "Reply to the first line of support tickets in our tone" is a job. "Use AI in support" is not.

## The main categories worth knowing

Most useful AI products fall into a few groups. Knowing the groups helps you compare like with like.

- **Agents and automation:** tools that take a goal and carry out several steps for you, such as researching, filling forms or updating a spreadsheet.
- **Writing and content:** drafting, editing and repurposing text, from emails to long articles.
- **Images and video:** creating product shots, ads and short videos without a studio.
- **Developer tools:** code completion, review and testing assistants.
- **Sales and support:** tools that qualify leads, summarise calls and draft replies.
- **Data and analytics:** tools that answer questions about your numbers in plain language.

## How to test a tool fairly

Run the same real task through two or three candidates. Do not use the vendor's demo. Use a task from last week, with your real messy inputs, and score each result on four things:

1. **Quality:** would you send it to a customer as it is?
2. **Editing time:** how long did you spend fixing it?
3. **Speed:** how long from request to usable result?
4. **Cost:** the price per month, and the price if your usage doubles.

Keep the scores in a simple sheet. After one afternoon you will usually have a clear winner, and a record you can show your team.

## Check the boring things before you pay

A good demo hides problems that show up in month three. Before you commit, look for:

- A free trial you can finish properly, not a ten-minute preview.
- Clear, public pricing, including what happens when you exceed limits.
- A way to **export your data**. A cheap tool you cannot leave becomes expensive later.
- An API or integrations, so the tool fits the way you already work.
- Where your data goes, and whether you can opt out of it being used to train models.
- A visible changelog or recent product updates, which shows the team is still building.

## Watch for lock-in and tool sprawl

Every new subscription is a small tax on your attention. Review your tools every quarter and cancel anything the team does not use weekly. Prefer tools that connect to the ones you already have over tools that want to replace everything.

If two tools overlap, keep the one your team actually opens. The best tool on paper loses to the tool people use.

## A simple monthly routine

You do not need to follow every launch. A light routine is enough:

1. Once a month, spend one hour scanning new tools in the two or three categories that matter to you.
2. Shortlist at most two.
3. Test them on a real task.
4. Adopt one, or none.
5. Remove one old tool for every new one you add.
