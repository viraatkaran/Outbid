Most diners decide where to eat before they ever open a menu. They search on their phone, glance at a handful of photos, a star rating and whether a place is open right now, then pick one. Getting more restaurant customers online means winning that quick decision, consistently, rather than hoping people notice your sign from the street. None of this requires a big marketing budget — it requires the basics done properly and a clear read on which channels actually fill tables.

## Start with a Google Business Profile built for food, not just a shop

A restaurant's free Google listing works harder than almost any other marketing you can do, but only if it is set up for how people actually search.

- **Add a real, current menu**, not a link to a PDF nobody opens on a phone. [Google's own menu editor](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9455840) lets you add items, sections and prices directly to the profile, and changes can take a day or two to appear on Maps and Search, so update it as soon as a dish or price changes rather than the night before a big weekend.
- **Keep hours, holidays and service options accurate** — dine-in, takeaway, delivery and reservations. A profile that says "open" when the kitchen has already closed loses the booking to whichever competitor got it right.
- **Upload real photos regularly**: the dining room, a signature dish, the exterior at night. Search platforms treat a profile that keeps adding fresh images very differently from one that has not changed in a year.
- **Pick the specific category and cuisine attributes** that match what you actually serve, not just "Restaurant." Specific categories help you show up for searches like "ramen near me" instead of only generic ones.

For the wider version of this checklist, see our [local SEO checklist for small businesses](/blog/local-seo-checklist-for-small-businesses) — most of it applies directly to a restaurant or cafe.

## Decide how you'll handle delivery apps before they decide for you

Third-party delivery marketplaces can bring in diners who have never heard of you, but they are not free traffic. [DoorDash's own published pricing](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-us/products/pricing), for example, lists delivery commissions of 15% to 30% depending on the plan, with its direct Online Ordering product priced separately and commission-free aside from standard payment processing. Whatever platforms you use, the commission structure is public — read it before committing.

A workable approach many independent restaurants settle on:

1. Use one or two marketplaces to get discovered by new customers, especially early on.
2. Put a clear, working "order direct" link or QR code on receipts, tables and your own Google profile and website, since a direct order avoids the marketplace cut entirely.
3. Reserve loyalty perks (a free side, a stamp card, early access to a new menu) for people who order direct, so the incentive to skip the marketplace is real rather than just suggested.
4. Review your order mix every few months and adjust rather than assuming the balance that worked last year still does.

Neither channel is automatically right or wrong — it depends on your margins and how much of your trade is new versus repeat customers.

## Build trust the same way diners build it among themselves

Nobody can taste a dish from a photo, so a restaurant's online reputation does most of the persuading before anyone walks in.

- **Ask for a review right after a good experience**, ideally with a simple link from the table or receipt, and reply to every review, good or bad, specifically rather than with a copy-pasted thank you.
- **Never buy reviews, write your own, or offer a discount explicitly in exchange for a review** — platforms detect patterns like this, and a suspended profile costs far more than any single review was worth.
- **Show real photos of real food and real diners** (with permission) instead of stock imagery. A slightly imperfect photo of an actual plate tends to perform better than a polished stock shot that looks like every other listing.
- **Handle a bad review calmly and specifically.** Acknowledge what happened, say what you're doing about it, and move the detailed back-and-forth offline. Future diners read how you respond more closely than the complaint itself.

## Show up in the searches people haven't typed into Google yet

A growing share of diners are starting their search in places besides a Google results page — asking a chat assistant, scrolling short videos, or reading a short list a friend shared. [DoorDash's 2026 restaurant SEO guide](https://merchants.doordash.com/en-us/blog/restaurant-seo-2026), citing the company's own Trends Report, describes a meaningful minority of diners now using AI chat tools to help pick where to eat, alongside traditional search. You cannot fully control what an AI assistant recommends, but the same groundwork helps across all of these surfaces: a complete, accurate profile; a real menu with real prices; consistent details across your website and listings; and a steady base of specific, recent reviews that describe actual dishes and experiences. Thin, inconsistent or outdated information is exactly what gets skipped, by a person scrolling quickly and by a system summarizing options.

Being listed somewhere diners already browse by category also helps. You can see how restaurants are ranked in [the United States](/c/united-states/restaurants) and in [the United Kingdom](/c/united-kingdom/restaurants), and a category-level spot for your own country is a low-cost way to appear alongside restaurants people are already comparing.

## Market for the next few weeks, not just today

Restaurant demand moves with the calendar: end-of-year parties, a local festival, exam season near a university, the first cold snap that makes people want soup instead of salad. Plan content and offers two to four weeks ahead of the next shift rather than reacting to it:

- Post about holiday bookings, group menus or private dining before the season everyone else is also advertising it.
- Update your Google Business Profile with a seasonal post or temporary hours well before a holiday, not the morning of.
- If you cater, make sure a catering or large-group page exists and is easy to find — a surprising amount of catering demand starts as a search rather than a referral.

## Track what actually fills tables

Resist running every channel at once with no way to tell what worked. Pick two or three — your Google profile, one delivery app, one ranking or directory listing — and track them deliberately:

- Use a distinct phone number, booking link or discount code per channel where you can.
- Ask new customers, briefly, how they found you.
- After a month, weigh what each channel brought in against the time or commission it cost. Our explainer on [whether paid leaderboards actually work](/blog/paid-leaderboards-do-they-work) walks through exactly this kind of comparison, including how to read verified clicks against real bookings.
- Keep what earns its place and drop what does not, rather than keeping every channel out of habit.

Pick three things: fix your Google Business Profile's menu and hours, ask your last ten happy customers for a review, and put a direct-order link on every receipt.
